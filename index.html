Thoughts on Frictionless Design

By Hyunjin Kim

A CURSE OR A BLESSING?

(thoughts on frictionless design)

From barefoot hunter-gatherers to modern humans navigating a digital world, every chapter of human progress has brought the slow death of something once essential to our existence. As technology advances, digital products become increasingly efficient, accessible, and seamless. While these improvements have made our lives more convenient, our pursuit of efficiency may also be taking away experiences crucial to our individual growth. After all, if technology continues to eliminate uncertainty, effort, and mistakes, what opportunities will we have left to develop our own judgment and agency? As Valerie mentions in her response, are we being robbed of opportunities for growth? (Valerie, Week 9, after-class response).

Of course, that doesn’t mean we should keep all friction. As Don Norman once said, “An ideal door is one that as I walk up to it and walk through it I’m not even aware that I had opened the door” (Smithies Design, 2018). His point captures something fundamental about good design: we shouldn’t have to struggle with things that could be simple. And as Jasmine illustrates through her experience, friction can sometimes create frustration rather than meaningful engagement (Jasmine, Week 2, after-class response). But perhaps the important distinction isn’t between friction and frictionless design, but between friction that gets in our way and friction that gives us room to think.

This brings me to Crystal’s point about the relationship between usability and user agency (Crystal, Week 8, after-class response). Her perspective made me wonder: at what point does simplifying an interface begin to narrow the paths users can take? When designers make everything frictionless and obvious, they aren’t necessarily just removing obstacles. They may also be deciding which choices users encounter in the first place. And if we never have to consider other possibilities, how do we learn to make those decisions on our own? Think about GPS directions. We may arrive at the destination without any trouble, but could we find our way back without the guidance? I wonder whether designing for usability expands our agency or limits the very experiences that help us develop it.

So, is low-friction design a curse or a blessing for user agency and growth? Let’s picture a handful of doors. Designers can make the first door easy to open so people don’t feel shut out. But should they open every door for us? As Fan mentioned, “if we rely on AI for everything, we may become less independent and stop thinking for ourselves” (Fan Lin, Week 7, after-class response). Perhaps there’s value in leaving just enough friction to make people curious about the other doors. A door to a house, a room, or a garden? Maybe some doors should feel unfamiliar, inviting people to explore rather than simply follow the most obvious path.

People don’t need to walk through every door, but we do want to know they’re there. There’s value in discovering what a handle is, how it looks, and how it works, rather than having every door open for us automatically. Choosing the straightforward path is still a choice, but only if we’re aware that other paths exist. As Andrea commented, “we need to maintain control over our work […] rather than letting it make the decisions for us” (Andrea, Week 6, after-class response). So maybe the question isn’t whether frictionless design is good or bad, but whether designers can make things easier without quietly deciding how far users are able to go.
